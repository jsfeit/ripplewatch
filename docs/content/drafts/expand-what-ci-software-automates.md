"Is this actually useful, or is it just marketing?" is a fair question to ask about any competitive intelligence tool, this one included. Here's an honest answer, split into what actually gets automated and what doesn't.

## What gets fully automated

- Watching sources continuously: pricing pages, job boards, news and funding coverage. A person doing this by hand, across more than two or three competitors, is the actual bottleneck automation removes. Nobody keeps it up for a year.
- First-pass relevance triage: scoring a finding against your specific positioning so nobody has to read every raw update just to find out whether it matters.
- Pulling win/loss reasons your team already logs in a CRM into one consolidated, ranked view, instead of exporting a spreadsheet and re-reading it every quarter.
- Noticing change. Software can compare today's pricing page to last month's and tell you what moved. A person usually can't, because nobody remembers exactly what the page said.

## What still needs a person

- Deciding what to actually do about a finding. A pricing change flagged as relevant still needs a real strategic call. No scoring system makes that call for you.
- Writing the narrative on a battlecard. A system can flag that a card is stale. It can't write persuasive competitive positioning copy for a rep to say on a call.
- Judging tone and context in something like a review or a job posting. Automation surfaces the signal. A person still has to read it in context to know what it actually implies.
- Knowing your own market. A tool can only weigh what it's been told about your buyers and your deals. If that input is thin, the scoring will be generic.

## The part that sits in between

Some work is neither fully automated nor fully manual. The best example is the question you ask after you read a finding. "Does this threaten the two deals we have open?" used to mean an hour of digging through notes. An AI assistant connected to your competitor data and your deal history can answer it in seconds, but you still decide whether to believe the answer. Treat it as a fast first draft from a colleague, not a verdict.

## Five questions to ask any vendor

You can use these on us too:

1. What does it watch, specifically? Ask for the list of sources, not the word "signals".
2. How does it decide what matters to me? If the answer is a generic urgency scale, that's weaker than scoring against your own buyers and lost deals.
3. How does it handle competitors with no public price? Many early-stage companies don't publish one, so a tool that depends only on pricing pages will go quiet exactly where you need it.
4. What happens between alerts? A feed you have to check is another dashboard. Ask where the answer shows up.
5. What does it cost when I use it a lot? Published pricing, with usage separated from the platform fee, is easier to plan around than a custom quote.

## How to find out in a week

Don't take a claim at face value either way. Pick the two or three competitors you worry about most, run the tool against them for a week, and check three things: Did it surface anything you didn't already know? Was the ranking roughly right? Did it save you real time? If the answer to all three is "not really", it isn't for you yet, and that's a fine result.

The honest pitch for any tool in this category, including this one, is that it removes the parts that are pure legwork: watching, collecting, first-pass triage. It doesn't remove the parts that require judgment. If a tool's marketing implies it replaces strategy itself rather than the busywork underneath it, that's the specific claim worth being skeptical of.
