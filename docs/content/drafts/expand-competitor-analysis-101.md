If you're just getting started, you don't need software yet. You need a framework, a short list of real competitors, and an afternoon. The most common framework is SWOT, and it still holds up as a starting point. It's close to [the same core process MIT Sloan teaches](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/ideas-made-to-matter/how-to-use-competitive-insight) for turning raw competitive information into an actual decision.

Here's the version that works for a small team, in the order we'd do it.

## Step 1: Pick two or three competitors, not ten

The first mistake is a list of fifteen names. You won't keep fifteen current, and most of them don't matter to your deals. Start with the companies a buyer actually compares you to, the ones that come up in calls or lost deals. If that list isn't obvious yet, see [who to track and who to ignore](/blog/who-to-track-and-who-to-ignore) before going further.

## Step 2: Gather facts, not impressions

For each competitor, collect the same few things from public sources:

- Their pricing page, if they have one. Write down the plan names and the numbers. If there's no page, write down "no public price", because that tells you something too.
- Their job board. Ten open sales roles and no new engineering roles says one thing. The opposite says another.
- Their last few launches and press mentions.
- What reviewers say, good and bad.
- What your own buyers have said about them, especially in deals you lost.

Use the same list for every competitor so you can compare them later. Ten minutes per company is enough for a first pass.

## Step 3: Run the SWOT honestly

SWOT is four short lists. Write them for your company against each competitor:

- **Strengths:** what you genuinely do better than them, not what you'd like to be true.
- **Weaknesses:** where they'd actually win a deal against you today.
- **Opportunities:** segments or use cases they serve poorly, or not at all.
- **Threats:** what they could plausibly do that would hurt you, not just what they've already done.

A quick example, with a made-up company. Say you sell documentation software to small product teams, and your closest competitor is a big general workspace tool with a free tier. Your strength might be that decisions stay linked to the work. Your weakness is that the competitor is already installed at most of your prospects. The opportunity is teams who've outgrown free pages and want structure. The threat is that the competitor ships a templates feature and removes your edge.

Notice what makes that useful: every line is something a rep could act on. If a line is vague, rewrite it until it isn't.

## Step 4: Turn it into a comparison table

Once the SWOT exists, a simple side-by-side makes it usable. Pricing, positioning, one or two real differentiators each. It doesn't need to be more sophisticated than that. [Our own comparison pages](/compare) are built on exactly this structure, and the reason it works is that a rep can read it in thirty seconds before a call.

## Step 5: Decide what changes

This is the step most people skip. A framework nobody acts on is a document, not analysis. Before you close the file, write down three changes it implies: a line in the talk track, a question to ask in discovery, a deal currently in flight that needs a different approach. If it implies none, the analysis wasn't specific enough.

## Where a spreadsheet stops working

A SWOT and a comparison table are a snapshot. They're accurate the day they're written, and [stale the day something changes](/blog/why-competitive-intelligence-goes-stale), which for most competitors is sooner than anyone expects. A competitor reprices, hires a head of partnerships, or quietly drops a plan, and your table still says what it said in March.

The framework isn't the failure point. The failure point is that almost nobody goes back and re-checks it. You can set a calendar reminder and be diligent for a month. After that it slips, because re-checking five sources for three competitors is dull work and nothing breaks the day you skip it.

That's the point where continuous monitoring earns its keep. It doesn't replace the thinking above. It replaces the manual re-checking underneath it, so the table stays true without anyone remembering to refresh it.
