[Continuous monitoring solves collection](/blog/why-competitive-intelligence-should-always-be-on). It doesn't solve action on its own. A Slack channel full of alerts that nobody discusses is barely better than no monitoring at all. The fix isn't a better dashboard. It's a short, recurring meeting with an actual agenda.

This is the one we'd run, and the reasons behind each part.

## Before the meeting: five minutes of prep

One person owns the review. Their only prep job is to pull the week's High relevance findings and open the Momentum view. If you use an assistant, you can ask it directly: "What changed with our competitors this week, and which of it matters for our deals?" Having the answer written down before anyone walks in is what keeps thirty minutes from becoming sixty.

If nothing scored High this week, the owner says so in the invite and the meeting shrinks to the last ten minutes. A quiet week is a real result, not a failure.

## The agenda

- Minutes 0 to 5: review this week's High relevance findings only, not the full raw feed. If nothing scored High, say so and move on. That's a real and useful outcome too.
- Minutes 5 to 15: for each High finding, make one decision. Does this change a talk track, a deal currently in flight, or a roadmap conversation, or is it just worth having noted?
- Minutes 15 to 25: a quick Momentum check. Which competitors are heating up this week, and does that match what the room's gut feel already was? When it doesn't, that mismatch is usually the most useful five minutes of the meeting.
- Minutes 25 to 30: log any new win/loss reasons from deals that closed this week, so next week's Momentum and relevance scoring stay accurate instead of drifting on stale data.

## What a good decision sounds like

Vague outcomes are how these meetings die. "Keep an eye on it" isn't a decision. Compare:

- A competitor drops its entry plan to a lower price. A weak outcome: "Noted." A strong one: "Ask every prospect in the pipeline what they're comparing us to this week, and Dana updates the pricing line in the battlecard by Thursday."
- A competitor posts six new sales roles in one region. A weak outcome: "They're growing." A strong one: "Expect more pressure in that region next quarter, so we shift the two open accounts there to a faster follow-up."

Every item gets a name and a day. If it can't get either, it goes in the "noted" pile, which stays short on purpose.

## Who should be in the room

Whoever owns messaging and whoever's closest to actual deals, not a large cross-functional standing meeting. Three or four people is plenty. A rep who sits in the meeting also becomes the person who carries a finding back to the team, which is how it actually changes what gets said on calls.

## How it goes wrong

Three failure modes show up again and again:

- It turns into a status update. The giveaway is people reading the feed aloud. If the meeting has no decisions, cut it down or cancel it.
- There's no owner. Everyone assumes someone else prepared. Pick one person and rotate quarterly.
- The alerts are too noisy. If every week has ten "High" findings, the scoring is off. Fix the inputs: log more win/loss reasons and tighten what counts as a direct competitor.

## How to tell it's working

Count decisions, not meetings. After a month you should be able to point to a few specific things that changed because of the review: a talk track line, a deal approach, a conversation with product. If you can't, change the agenda before you change the tool.

## Why this sticks where a quarterly review doesn't

A thorough quarterly deep-dive is stale the day it's delivered, [the same failure mode that makes most CI programs go quiet after the initial push](/blog/why-competitive-intelligence-goes-stale). Thirty minutes a week compounds instead. It works directly off whatever's already landed in [the Slack channel scored alerts get pushed to](/blog/what-slack-native-competitive-intelligence-actually-looks-like) or the answer your assistant gives when you ask, so there's no separate report anyone has to prepare beforehand. Just a standing slot to talk about what already showed up.
